The silverback sat fifteen metres from us in the undergrowth, watching with a calm authority that made the word "primate" feel suddenly insufficient. He was enormous — perhaps 200 kilograms of muscle and intelligence arranged in a posture of absolute self-possession. Around him, six other gorillas moved through the vegetation, feeding, grooming, playing.

We stood in silence. The guide had asked us to make no sudden movements, to keep our voices low, to follow his lead at all times. None of this felt difficult. In the presence of a mountain gorilla, stillness comes naturally.

## The Climb

It had begun at dawn, at the park headquarters in Kinigi. Our group of eight — the maximum permitted per gorilla family — had received the briefing: walk in single file, do not eat or drink near the gorillas, maintain a minimum distance of seven metres, limit the visit to one hour. These rules exist not as bureaucratic convenience but as the result of careful scientific research into what protects gorilla health and habituates families without altering their behaviour.

The climb into the Virunga volcanoes takes one to four hours depending on where the gorillas are resting that day. Trackers set out each morning before dawn to locate each family and radio its position back to the guides. We climbed through eucalyptus plantation, then into the dense primary forest of the park boundary, following machete-cut paths through vegetation that closed behind us like water.

The porters — local villagers employed by the park — carried water, extra jackets, and the occasional guest who needed support on the steeper sections. Their knowledge of the mountain was intimate and their pace was measured. The oldest porter, a man named Emmanuel who had been making this climb for twenty years, pointed out plants with medicinal uses, bird calls overhead, and fresh gorilla dung that told him we were close.

## The Encounter

When we found them, the guide held up a fist and we stopped. Through the undergrowth we could see the silverback — Gihishamwotsi, the guide told us, a patriarch of fifteen years — and around him the rustling shapes of his family.

The guide led us in a careful arc, maintaining distance, moving into a position where we could observe without crowding. The gorillas, for their part, largely ignored us. Two juveniles played in the branches of a Hagenia tree above us, wrestling with the easy physicality of children who have not yet learned to be careful. A female sat nearby, nursing an infant perhaps six months old. The infant stared at us with enormous brown eyes.

For fifty minutes we watched and were watched. A young male — a blackback, not yet the silver of full maturity — approached to within five metres, sat, and began grooming himself, glancing at us occasionally with what seemed like mild curiosity. I thought about what it would take to habituate a wild animal to human presence so completely that it could sit this close without alarm, and what care and patience that process must require.

## The Conservation Story

Rwanda's mountain gorilla population has grown from around 620 in 2010 to over 1,000 today — the only great ape population that is increasing. This is a conservation success story built on a particular model: high-value tourism, rigorous veterinary monitoring, community benefit-sharing, and absolute commitment from the Rwandan government.

The permit system — each permit costs $1,500, with funds distributed to conservation and local communities — is deliberately exclusive. It limits pressure on the gorillas while generating revenue that makes conservation economically competitive with alternative land uses. The communities around the park receive a percentage of all permit revenue, creating a direct incentive for local people to protect rather than poach.

It is a model that other African conservation efforts are studying closely.

## After

We descended in near-silence, each absorbed in our own processing of what we had seen. The mist had cleared by the time we reached the forest boundary, revealing the green patchwork of farms below and the blue distance of Lake Kivu on the horizon.

At the bottom, Emmanuel poured tea from a thermos he had been carrying for four hours. We sat on a wall and drank it without speaking, watching a pair of crowned cranes fly across the field boundary below us. The gorillas were already somewhere in the forest above, moving on to their afternoon feeding sites, indifferent to our departure.

I have made this climb four times. Each time, the hour in the forest changes something I cannot easily name. Each time, I descend understanding — more than I did before — why this place, and these animals, must be protected at any cost.

*Sarah Chen is a wildlife journalist and expedition guide who has worked with Adventure International for seven years.*
