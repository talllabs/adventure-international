The wave at Scorpion Bay arrived like a promise kept. It stood up off the point with the unhurried confidence of a wave that knows exactly what it intends to do, then peeled left across four distinct sections for what felt — impossibly — like four hundred metres. In thirty years of surfing, I have never ridden a wave that long.

Baja California is one of surfing's great secrets. Less celebrated than Indonesia, less crowded than Central America, it sits just below the California border and yet feels a world away from the beachside sprawl of San Diego and Los Angeles. The peninsula stretches 1,247 kilometres south into the Pacific, and along its Pacific coast, consistent northwest swells wrap into dozens of point breaks, beach breaks, and remote reef set-ups that receive almost no visitors for most of the year.

## The Route South

We left Tijuana in two trucks loaded with boards, camping gear, and enough provisions for a week. The plan was loose: drive south, surf everything that looked good, sleep where we stopped. California Journeys has been running versions of this trip for eight years, and the guides know the peninsula intimately — which breaks fire on which swells, where to camp out of the wind, which of the small fishing towns have good ceviche and cold beer.

The first night we camped on a bluff above a perfect left-hand point. The waves were small — two feet, maybe three — but the shape was immaculate, and we surfed until the light gave out. Around a fire, we watched the Milky Way emerge and listened to the Pacific working below us.

## Bahía Tortugas

Three days south, we reached Bahía Tortugas — a small fishing town halfway down the peninsula where the local fishermen still pull in enormous hauls of lobster and where the waves outside the bay break with a power that surprised us.

Our guide, Jorge, grew up here. He knows every reef, every current, every shift in the wind that changes the character of the break. With him in the water, we surfed spots that receive perhaps a handful of visiting surfers each year, waves that felt entirely ours.

Evenings in Bahía Tortugas meant fish tacos from the stall near the dock, cold Pacifico beer, and conversations with the fishermen who watched us with the polite bafflement that locals everywhere reserve for surfers.

## Scorpion Bay

Scorpion Bay — San Juanico — is the reason serious surfers make the journey. When a proper northwest swell runs, the main point here can produce waves of extraordinary length, wrapping around the headland across four linked sections that connect on the best days into a single, four-hundred-metre ride.

We had two days of that swell. Two days of paddling out through the channel, catching waves, riding them until they finally gave out in the inside bay, and paddling back to do it again. My arms gave out before my enthusiasm did.

## Why Baja, Why Now

There are people who will tell you that the good surf destinations are gone — overrun, over-documented, over-crowded. Baja is evidence against that argument. The peninsula is long, the roads are rough, and most people don't have the time or the inclination to drive four hundred kilometres on dirt to reach a wave that might or might not be working.

That is exactly why we go.
